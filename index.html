Situational Leadership & Follower Development

1. Banner & Introduction

Welcome to Week 3 of DEL 5807: Leadership Foundations! Over the past two weeks, we examined individual leadership traits, authentic identity, and core values. This week, we introduce a crucial pivot: moving from individual self-reflection to situational flexibility and follower development.

In complex organizational environments, executive effectiveness is rarely about applying a single preferred leadership style. Instead, as Blanchard's SLII® model demonstrates, high-performing leaders continuously evaluate follower development (competence and commitment) on specific tasks and dynamically calibrate their combination of directive and supportive behaviors. Furthermore, by linking situational leadership with Katz's Three-Skill Model, executives learn how to elevate their strategic focus while providing appropriate operational guidance.

2. Module Key Takeaways

  1. 1Leadership is Goal-Specific, Not Fixed: Effective leadership is contingent on matching your behavior (Directive vs. Supportive) to the specific development level (Competence vs. Commitment) of a follower on a given task.
  2. 2Bridging Situational Demands with Katz's Skills Model: As leaders ascend into executive roles, technical execution must yield to conceptual strategy and human interaction. However, when followers face new or complex tasks (D1/D2), executives must temporarily re-engage directive or coaching behaviors.
  3. 3The Danger of Style Misalignment: Over-directing highly competent followers (D4) causes micromanagement and burnout, while under-directing developing followers (D1) leads to operational error and frustration.

3. Section 1: The SLII® Framework – Leadership Styles & Development Levels

The Four Leadership Styles (S1–S4)

S1 – Directing:

High directive, low supportive behavior. The leader gives explicit instructions, establishes goals, and closely supervises execution.

S2 – Coaching:

High directive, high supportive behavior. The leader provides directions and makes final decisions while soliciting feedback, encouraging input, and supporting emotional needs.

S3 – Supporting:

High supportive, low directive behavior. The leader facilitates problem-solving, listens, praises, and delegates day-to-day decision-making authority to the follower.

S4 – Delegating:

Low supportive, low directive behavior. The leader delegates full responsibility for task execution and decision-making to a highly capable follower.

The Four Development Levels (D1–D4)

Choose the leadership style that fits each follower’s development level on the task. Check each match for feedback.

D3 – Moderate-High Competence / Variable Commitment:

Moderate to high competence, variable commitment (e.g., capable performer lacking self-confidence or alignment).

D1 – Low Competence / High Commitment:

Low competence, high commitment (e.g., enthusiastic beginner needing clear direction).

D4 – High Competence / High Commitment:

High competence, high commitment (e.g., seasoned expert fully capable of autonomous execution).

D2 – Some Competence / Low Commitment:

Low to some competence, low commitment (e.g., disillusioned learner needing direction and encouragement).

4. Section 2: Integrating Katz's Three-Skill Model with Situational Leadership

In Chapter 3, Robert Katz outlines three essential administrative skills: Technical, Human, and Conceptual. Executive leadership demands a clear shift in how these skills are deployed across situational demands:

Technical Skill Integration:

Critical at lower operational levels, but decreases in direct operational importance for top executives. However, when managing D1/D2 followers on technical tasks, leaders must possess enough technical understanding to set clear standards.

Human Skill Integration:

Paramount across all organizational levels and all development stages (D1–D4). Effective human skills enable leaders to listen, build trust, provide constructive feedback, and assess follower readiness accurately.

Conceptual Skill Integration:

Vital at upper executive levels. Conceptual skills allow leaders to formulate overall vision and strategic direction, empowering D3 and D4 followers to execute autonomously while maintaining systemic alignment.

5. Section 3: Diagnostic Precision & Strengths/Criticisms of SLII®

Strengths of the Situational Approach

  • Marketplace Practicality: Widely validated in corporate training (used by over 10,000 organizations and Fortune 500 companies).
  • Prescriptive Value: Offers explicit 'if-then' guidance for matching leadership behavior to follower readiness.
  • Dynamic Flexibility: Rejects one-size-fits-all leadership, requiring executives to adjust behaviors dynamic to changing project phases.

Criticisms & Theoretical Caveats

  • Limited Empirical Research: Sparse published research verifying the exact theoretical progression of commitment drops from D1 to D2.
  • Group vs. Individual Dynamics: The model does not fully address how executives adapt styles simultaneously when leading groups with diverse individual readiness levels.
  • Demographic Variables: Fails to account for how age, gender, education, and cultural norms influence follower preferences for direction versus support.
